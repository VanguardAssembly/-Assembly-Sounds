about:
The Vijñāna Bhairava is a short tantra from Kashmir, a dialogue between the god Bhairava (Shiva) and the goddess Bhairavī. She asks what his true nature is. He answers that it cannot be described, only entered. Then he gives her 112 *dhāraṇās*, brief ways in. Each takes an ordinary moment and turns it into a doorway to pure, open awareness: the pause between two breaths, a sound fading away, the sky, a sneeze, a sudden joy, the edge of sleep.

The great Kashmiri philosophers Abhinavagupta and Kṣemarāja quoted it. In 1957 a free English version by Paul Reps and Nyogen Senzaki, called "Centering", carried it into Zen and Western meditation circles. The Vijñāna Bhairava session uses five of its doorways.

## The question
The goddess said: Lord, what is your true nature? Is it sound, or a circle of letters, or the energies of the three powers? How can it be known?

Bhairava said: My true form is not any of these. They are told to the immature, to lead them on, as a mother uses sweets to coax a child. My real nature is fullness, free of space and time, of direction and description. It cannot be told, only lived. Here are the ways to enter it.

## The turning breath
The breath goes out and the breath comes in. Fix the mind on the two places where it turns, and the fullness of Bhairava appears.

When the breath has gone out and has not yet come in, or has come in and not yet gone out, rest in that pause. In that middle, the essence shines.

## Sound fading into silence
Listen to the long sound of a stringed instrument, or of any instrument, all the way to its end. When the mind is absorbed in it until it fades, one becomes the supreme space.

Chant Om slowly, and attend to the silence at the end of the sound. In that emptiness, one becomes empty and full.

## The empty body
Imagine the body as empty in every direction, like a vessel of space with only skin for its walls. Hold that, and the mind dissolves into what has no thought.

## The open sky
Gaze into the clear, cloudless sky without moving the eyes, until the mind is as still and open as the sky itself. Then one takes on the form of Bhairava.

## The edge of sleep
When sleep has not yet come and the outer world has already faded, at that edge, the supreme goddess shows herself.

## The breath's own mantra
The breath goes out and comes back in with the two sounds *ha* and *sa*. So, day and night, every living being repeats the mantra *haṃsa*, twenty-one thousand six hundred times. This is the japa of the goddess, easy for the wise and hard for the dull.

by: the Upanishads and tantras

about:
*So'ham* means "I am That"; reversed, *haṃsa* also means "swan", the bird that stands for the free soul. The yoga and tantra texts call it the *ajapā*, the "unrecited" mantra. Nobody has to say it, because the breath already does. *Sa* is the sound of the breath coming in, *ha* (or *ham*) the breath going out. They say every person repeats it about 21,600 times a day, the number of breaths in twenty-four hours at fifteen a minute.

The words *so 'ham asmi*, "I am He", first appear at the end of the Īśā Upanishad, where the dying sage sees the sun and knows himself one with its spirit. The practice of hearing the mantra in the breath is taught in the Vijñāna Bhairava, in the Dhyānabindu and Haṃsa Upanishads, and by modern teachers such as Swami Muktananda. Most give *so* on the in-breath and *ham* on the out-breath. A few lineages reverse it, as *haṃsa*. The So'ham session simply listens to the breath saying it.

## I am He (Īśā Upaniṣad 16)
O Sun, the one seer, the nourisher, part your rays and gather your light. I see your most beautiful form. That person over there, in the sun: I am He.

## The breath's japa (as the Dhyānabindu Upaniṣad gives it)
With *ha* it goes out, and with *sa* it comes in again. *Haṃsa, haṃsa*: so the living soul repeats this mantra always, twenty-one thousand six hundred times, day and night.

## How to practise
1. Sit comfortably and let the breath move by itself.
2. As it comes in, hear *so* silently.
3. As it goes out, hear *ham*.
4. Do not say it; notice that the breath is already saying it.
5. Rest in the small pauses where one breath turns into the next, where the mantra falls silent.

The *Ṣaṭ-cakra-nirūpaṇa* is a 1577 Bengali tantric text. In fifty-five verses it describes the six *cakras*, "wheels" or "centres", along the central channel of the subtle body, and the thousand-petalled lotus above the head. For each it gives the number and colour of the petals, the element, the seed syllable (*bīja*), the presiding deities and the animal.

At the base sleeps *kuṇḍalinī*, "the coiled one", pictured as a serpent wrapped three and a half times around a liṅga. In awakening she rises through the centres to the crown, where Shakti unites with Shiva. In 1919 the British judge Sir John Woodroffe, writing as Arthur Avalon, translated it as *The Serpent Power*. Almost every later book on the chakras, Eastern or Western, depends on it.

The seed-sound, kuṇḍalinī and pineal sessions use its system. The rainbow colours now usual in the West are a 20th-century addition, explained under "the modern chakra system".

## The six centres and the crown
- **Mūlādhāra**, root support: at the base of the spine. Four crimson petals; element earth, a yellow square; seed syllable **LAM**; the elephant Airāvata. Here kuṇḍalinī sleeps.
- **Svādhiṣṭhāna**, her own place: at the root of the genitals. Six vermilion petals; element water, a white crescent moon; seed syllable **VAM**; the makara, a sea creature.
- **Maṇipūra**, city of jewels: at the navel. Ten petals the colour of rain clouds; element fire, a red triangle; seed syllable **RAM**; the ram.
- **Anāhata**, unstruck: at the heart. Twelve deep-red petals; element air, a smoky six-pointed star; seed syllable **YAM**; the black antelope. Here the "unstruck sound" is heard.
- **Viśuddha**, purified: at the throat. Sixteen smoky-purple petals; element space, a white full moon; seed syllable **HAM**; a white elephant.
- **Ājñā**, command: between the eyebrows. Two white petals; the mind; seed syllable **OM**. Here the guru's command is received.
- **Sahasrāra**, thousand-petalled: above the crown of the head, beyond the elements, the seat of Shiva, where kuṇḍalinī comes to rest.

## Kuṇḍalinī asleep
In the root centre, coiled around the self-born liṅga, sleeps the goddess Kuṇḍalinī, fine as a lotus fibre and bright as lightning. With her mouth she covers the door of Brahman, the entrance to the central channel. She is the beguiler of the world, and her soft humming is like the drone of bees in love.

## The rising
When she is awakened, she pierces the centres one by one, dissolving each element into the next finer one, earth into water, water into fire, fire into air, air into space and space into mind, until she joins her lord in the thousand-petalled lotus.

orig: श्रीरुद्रम्, Śrī Rudram (Taittirīya Saṃhitā 4.5 and 4.7); नमः शिवाय, Namaḥ Śivāya (Sanskrit)

about:
The *Śrī Rudram* is one of the oldest and most sacred Vedic hymns, still chanted daily in Shiva temples across India. In eleven sections it salutes Rudra, the fierce and healing god who would become Shiva, in his hundred forms. It names him the lord of every place and every being: of trees and roads, of thieves and craftsmen, of mountains, rivers and storms. It asks him to be gentle, *śiva*, "kind".

In its eighth section, the very heart of the hymn, stand the words *namaḥ śivāya ca śivatarāya ca*: "salutation to the kind one, and to the kinder still". From them comes the *pañcākṣara*, the five-syllable mantra *Na-maḥ Śi-vā-ya*, "I bow to Shiva". With *Om* before it, *Om Namaḥ Śivāya* is the great mantra of Shaivism, repeated on a mālā of 108 rudrākṣa beads. In the Śaiva Siddhānta tradition the five syllables are read as the five elements: earth, water, fire, air and space. The Om Namah Shivaya session is japa of this mantra.

## The heart of the Rudram (Taittirīya Saṃhitā 4.5.8)
= namaḥ śambhave ca mayobhave ca namaḥ śaṅkarāya ca mayaskarāya ca namaḥ śivāya ca śivatarāya ca
Salutation to the source of happiness, and to the source of delight. Salutation to the one who makes peace, and to the one who brings joy. Salutation to the kind one, Shiva, and to the kinder still.

## The opening (Taittirīya Saṃhitā 4.5.1)
Salutation to your anger, Rudra, and to your arrow; salutation to your bow and to your two arms. With that form of yours which is kind, Rudra, gentle and free from harm, look on us and bring us happiness.

## The mantra
= Om Namaḥ Śivāya
Om, I bow to Shiva: to the kind one, the auspicious, the inner Self.

@ gayatri
title: The Gāyatrī Mantra
orig: गायत्री, Gāyatrī, also called Sāvitrī (Sanskrit); Ṛg Veda 3.62.10
by: the seer Viśvāmitra
date: c. 1200–1000 BCE

about:
The Gāyatrī is the best-loved verse of the Ṛg Veda, the oldest Hindu scripture. It is named after its metre, *gāyatrī*, three lines of eight syllables. It is a prayer to Savitṛ, the divine power of the sun that wakes and moves all things, to stir and light up our thinking.

Traditionally it is said at the *sandhyās*, the "joinings" of day and night, at dawn and dusk and often at noon, with water offered to the sun. It is prefixed by *Om* and the three *vyāhṛtis*, the names of the three worlds: earth, atmosphere and heaven. For centuries it was taught only to initiated men of the upper castes. Since the 19th century, reform movements such as the Arya Samaj have opened it to everyone, and it is now chanted by millions. The Gāyatrī session follows the sandhyā custom.

## The mantra
= Om bhūr bhuvaḥ svaḥ
= tat savitur vareṇyaṃ
= bhargo devasya dhīmahi
= dhiyo yo naḥ pracodayāt
> Om. Earth, atmosphere, heaven.
> Let us meditate on that most excellent radiance of the divine Savitṛ, the sun who gives life.
> May he stir our minds and light up our thoughts.

## The word by word
- **tat**: that
- **savitur vareṇyam**: of Savitṛ, the most choice, the most worthy
- **bhargaḥ**: radiance, splendour that burns away
- **devasya dhīmahi**: of the god, we meditate on
- **dhiyaḥ yaḥ naḥ pracodayāt**: who may stir our thoughts, our visions, our understanding

## When to say it (Manusmṛti 2.101)
At the dawn twilight let him stand, repeating the Sāvitrī until the sun is seen. At the evening twilight let him sit, repeating it until the stars appear.

by: a late Vaiṣṇava Upanishad; the chanting spread by Chaitanya Mahāprabhu

fam: hindu

about:
A very short Upanishad in which the sage Nārada asks Brahmā how people can cross over the evils of the Kali age, the dark age we now live in. Brahmā's answer is to repeat the names of God. He gives the sixteen-word *mahā-mantra*, "great mantra": *Hare Rāma* and *Hare Kṛṣṇa*, each repeated. He says it destroys the sixteen coverings of the soul, and that no rules bind it: anyone, at any time, may chant it.

In 16th-century Bengal the ecstatic saint Chaitanya Mahāprabhu made the names the centre of a new devotional movement. Its people sang them in the streets in great processions of *kīrtan*, with drums and hand cymbals. In 1966 A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda founded the International Society for Krishna Consciousness (ISKCON) in New York, and the mantra went round the world. Its members chant sixteen rounds of 108 on beads each day. The Upanishad gives the names beginning with *Hare Rāma*; Chaitanya's followers begin with *Hare Kṛṣṇa*. The Mahā-mantra session offers both japa and kīrtan.

## Nārada's question and Brahmā's answer
At the end of the third age Nārada went to Brahmā and said: "Lord, how can I wander the earth and cross over the Kali age?" Brahmā said: "Well asked! Hear the secret that all the Vedas keep: by speaking the name of the Lord Nārāyaṇa, the first person, one shakes off the evils of the Kali age."

## The sixteen names
= Hare Rāma Hare Rāma Rāma Rāma Hare Hare
= Hare Kṛṣṇa Hare Kṛṣṇa Kṛṣṇa Kṛṣṇa Hare Hare
These sixteen names destroy the evils of the Kali age. No better way is seen in all the Vedas.

## No rules
Nārada asked: "What are the rules for it?" Brahmā said: "There are no rules. Whether pure or impure, whoever always speaks it reaches the world of the Lord, his nearness, his form and union with him."

## How to chant (Chaitanya, Śikṣāṣṭakam 3)
= tṛṇād api sunīcena taror api sahiṣṇunā / amāninā mānadena kīrtanīyaḥ sadā hariḥ
Lower than a blade of grass, more patient than a tree, claiming no honour and giving honour to all: so one should always sing the name of Hari.

@ japa
title: Japa: repeating a mantra on a mālā
orig: जप, japa, "muttering, repeating"; माला, mālā, "garland" (Sanskrit)
by: Hindu, Buddhist, Jain and Sikh practice; the Laws of Manu, the Gītā and the Yoga Sūtras
date: from the Vedic age onward
fam: hindu
kind: teaching
credit: Assembly Sounds' own renderings and summary from the Sanskrit.
more: Swami Sivananda, *Japa Yoga* (1952).
more: Harvey P. Alper, ed., *Understanding Mantras* (1989).
about:
Japa is the repetition of a mantra or divine name: spoken, whispered or silent. It is perhaps the most widespread meditation in the world. Hindus, Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs all use a *mālā* of beads to count it, and the rosary of Christians and the *misbaḥa* of Muslims are close relatives.

The Laws of Manu rank the three kinds: spoken japa is good, whispered japa a hundred times better, and silent japa a thousand times better. In the Bhagavad Gītā Krishna says that "of offerings I am the offering of japa". Patañjali makes the repetition of Om, with attention to its meaning, a direct path to the inner self.

A Hindu mālā has 108 beads and one larger bead, the *meru* or guru bead, which is never crossed. At the end of a round you turn the mālā and go back. It is usually held in the right hand and moved with the thumb over the middle finger. Many say the index finger, which stands for the ego, should not touch it. Shaivas use rudrākṣa seeds, and Vaishnavas beads of tulasī wood. The japa sessions follow these customs.

## Three kinds of japa (Manusmṛti 2.85)
The offering of japa is ten times better than the prescribed offerings. Whispered japa is a hundred times better, and japa in the mind a thousand times.

## In the Gītā and the Yoga Sūtras
Among offerings I am the offering of japa. (Bhagavad Gītā 10.25)

Its word is Om. Repeat it, and dwell on its meaning. (Yoga Sūtras 1.27–28)

## Why 108?
Many reasons are given: the 27 lunar mansions times the four quarters of each; the 12 signs of the zodiac times the 9 planets; or simply the sacred number itself, written 1-0-8, one, nothing and infinity. Whatever the reason, 108 has been sacred in India for well over a thousand years.

## How to use a mālā
1. Hold the mālā in the right hand, draped over the middle finger.
2. Start at the bead next to the guru bead.
3. Say the mantra once, and with the thumb draw the next bead towards you.
4. After 108, when you reach the guru bead, do not cross it. Pause, then turn the mālā round and begin the next round.

@ satyananda
title: Yoga Nidra and Antar Mouna: the Bihar School methods
by: Swami Satyananda Saraswati
date: *Meditations from the Tantras* 1974; *Yoga Nidra* 1976
fam: hindu
kind: manual
credit: Summarised in Assembly Sounds' own words; Swami Satyananda's books are still under copyright and are not quoted.
more: Swami Satyananda Saraswati, *Yoga Nidra* (Bihar School of Yoga, 1976).
more: Swami Satyananda Saraswati, *Meditations from the Tantras* (1974).
about:
Swami Satyananda Saraswati (1923–2009) was a disciple of Swami Sivananda of Rishikesh. In 1963–64 he founded the Bihar School of Yoga at Munger, on the Ganges, and it trained teachers from all over the world. He gave old tantric practices a clear modern form, and two of them are in this app.

*Yoga nidra*, "yogic sleep", is a guided practice done lying down. You stay aware while the body sleeps. Satyananda drew it from the tantric practice of *nyāsa*, placing awareness or mantras on parts of the body. Its fixed sequence starts with a resolve, the *saṅkalpa*. Then come a rotation of attention round the body, breath counting, pairs of opposite sensations and a series of images. You make the resolve again and return. NSDR, "non-sleep deep rest", is its modern secular name.

*Antar mouna*, "inner silence", is a sitting practice of witnessing. First you watch sounds and the senses. Then you watch spontaneous thoughts, then make and dismiss a chosen thought, then let go of thoughts one by one, and finally rest in the gaps where no thought is. The yoga nidra and antar mouna sessions follow these structures.

## Yoga nidra, in its stages
1. **Preparation**: lie on the back, still and comfortable, and resolve to stay awake.
2. **Saṅkalpa**: make a short, positive resolve in your own words, and repeat it three times.
3. **Rotation of consciousness**: move the attention quickly round the body in a fixed order, one part at a time, without moving.
4. **Breath awareness**: watch the natural breath, or count breaths backwards.
5. **Opposite sensations**: feel heaviness and lightness, warmth and coolness, in turn.
6. **Visualisation**: let a series of simple images pass before the mind's eye.
7. **Saṅkalpa again**: repeat the resolve three times, now in the deep, receptive state.
8. **Return**: become aware of the body and the room, move slowly, and sit up.

## Antar mouna, in its stages
1. Witness sounds and sensations, without naming or judging them.
2. Witness spontaneous thoughts, like watching a film.
3. Create a thought on purpose, think it through, then deliberately throw it out.
4. Watch spontaneous thoughts again, and let each one go as it arises.
5. Rest in thoughtlessness: notice the gaps between thoughts and stay in them.

Venkataraman Iyer (1879–1950) was a Tamil schoolboy of sixteen when, alone in his uncle's house, he was seized by an overwhelming fear of death. Lying still, he enacted his own death, and found that the "I" was untouched by it. A few weeks later he left home for the holy mountain Arunachala at Tiruvannamalai and never left it again. He sat for years in silence, in temples and caves. Visitors called him Ramana Maharshi, "the great seer", and an ashram grew around him.

His method is *ātma vichāra*, "self-inquiry". You do not suppress thoughts. Each time one arises you ask: "To whom does this thought come?" The answer, "to me", turns the attention back to the feeling of "I" itself. Then you ask, "Who am I?" Held steadily, the question takes the mind back to its source, the pure awareness the Upanishads call the Self. The Self-Inquiry session follows his short answers in *Who Am I?*.

## Who am I?
I am not this body, made of the seven elements. I am not the five senses, hearing, touch, sight, taste and smell, nor the five organs of action. I am not the five vital breaths, nor even the mind that thinks. When all of these have been set aside, "not this, not this", the awareness that alone remains: that I am.

## What is that awareness?
Its nature is being, consciousness and bliss.

## How is the mind to be quieted?
By the inquiry "Who am I?". The thought "Who am I?" will destroy all other thoughts. Like the stick used to stir a funeral fire, it will itself be burned up in the end. Then there is the realisation of the Self.

## What to do when thoughts come
When other thoughts arise, do not follow them. Ask instead: "To whom do they come?" However many thoughts come, it does not matter. As each one appears, ask, "To whom has this thought come?" The answer will be, "To me". Then ask, "Who am I?", and the mind will turn back to its source, and the thought will subside. With repeated practice the mind grows strong in abiding at its source.

## Steadiness is the only effort
The mind will only subside through inquiry. Other methods may quiet it for a while, but it rises again. Hold to the inquiry until the "I" itself dissolves.

about:
A hymn of one hundred verses to the goddess Tripurasundarī, "the Beautiful One of the Three Worlds", also called Lalitā. It is the best-loved poem of Śrī Vidyā, the tantric worship of the goddess as supreme consciousness. Its first verses teach that Shiva, pure awareness, can do nothing without Shakti, his power. Later verses describe the *Śrī Yantra* (or Śrī Cakra), her geometric form and dwelling.

The Śrī Yantra is made of nine interlocking triangles: four pointing up, for Shiva, and five pointing down, for Shakti. Together they form forty-three smaller triangles around a central point, the *bindu*. Rings of eight and sixteen lotus petals, three circles and a square with four gates surround them. Worship moves from the outer square inward, circuit by circuit, to the bindu, where Shiva and Shakti are one. The Śrī Yantra session makes this journey to the centre.

## Shiva and Shakti (verse 1)
Only when Shiva is joined with Shakti does he have the power to create. Without her, the god cannot even stir. So how could anyone who has not gathered great merit bow to you or praise you, whom even Viṣṇu, Shiva and Brahmā worship?

## The Śrī Cakra (verse 11)
With four triangles of Shiva and five of Shakti, distinct from them, the nine roots of the world; with the eight-petalled lotus and the sixteen-petalled lotus, three circles and three lines: so the corners of your dwelling come to forty-four.

The forty-four are usually counted as the forty-three small triangles and the central point.

## The journey inward
1. **The square** with four gates: the world of the senses.
2. **The three circles** and **the lotuses** of sixteen and eight petals: the mind growing quieter.
3. **The circuits of triangles**, from fourteen down to ten, ten, eight and one: the powers of the goddess.
4. **The bindu**: the point at the centre, where Shiva and Shakti are one, pure, still awareness.

about:
Maharishi Mahesh Yogi (c. 1918–2008) was a disciple of Swami Brahmananda Saraswati, the Śaṅkarācārya of Jyotirmath in the Himalayas. From 1955 he taught a simple mantra meditation in India, and from 1958 around the world. It became famous when the Beatles studied with him at Rishikesh in 1968.

In TM a teacher gives each student a mantra, a meaningless sound from the Vedic tradition. It is thought effortlessly, without concentrating, for twenty minutes twice a day, sitting comfortably with the eyes closed. When you notice you have drifted into thoughts, you come back to the mantra easily, without forcing anything. The Maharishi taught that the mind then naturally settles to quieter levels of thinking until it "transcends" thought altogether. TM is taught in a paid seven-step course, and its mantras are kept private.

It is the most studied meditation in the world. Wallace's 1970 study in *Science* found lower oxygen use, lower heart rate and more alpha brain waves during practice. In 2013 an American Heart Association statement said TM "may be considered" to lower blood pressure. It was the only form of meditation that the statement found had enough evidence to consider. Meta-analyses suggest it reduces anxiety, especially in very anxious people. Much of the research comes from researchers linked to the movement, and independent studies are fewer. The effortless mantra session is inspired by TM's method, but it is not TM, and it uses no TM mantra.

## The method in brief
- Sit comfortably, close the eyes, and rest for a moment.
- Think the mantra quietly and easily, without concentrating, without forcing a rhythm.
- When you notice you are thinking about something else, come back to the mantra, gently.
- Do not fight thoughts; they are part of the process.
- After twenty minutes, stop and sit with the eyes closed for two or three minutes before getting up.

Harbhajan Singh Puri (1929–2004), a Punjabi Sikh customs officer, came to Los Angeles in 1968 and began teaching what he called Kundalini Yoga to the young people of the counterculture. He took the name Yogi Bhajan and founded the 3HO, the "Healthy, Happy, Holy Organization", in 1969. His classes joined vigorous breathing and postures, held for minutes at a time, with Sikh mantras and long chanting meditations. Every class opened by "tuning in" with the Adi Mantra and closed with deep relaxation, often to the sound of a large gong.

He presented the system as an ancient secret tradition. Historians have shown that it is largely his own modern synthesis of haṭha yoga, Sikh devotional practice and other elements. In 2020 an independent investigation commissioned by the organisations he founded concluded that it was more likely than not that he had sexually abused and mistreated a number of his followers. Many teachers now teach the practices while acknowledging that history.

The Kundalini sessions in this app use only the gentle parts: chanting, mudrās, relaxation and the gong. They leave out Breath of Fire and breath retention. Breath of Fire appears only in the separate breathwork session, behind its warning.

## The mantras
- **Ong Namo Guru Dev Namo** (the Adi Mantra): "I bow to the creative wisdom of all; I bow to the divine teacher within." Chanted three times on one long breath to tune in.
- **Sat Nam**: "Truth is my identity", or "true name", from the opening of the Sikh Mūl Mantar. Chanted as a long *Saaat* and a short *Nam*, and used as a greeting.
- **Ra Ma Da Sa, Sa Say So Hung** (the Siri Gaitri Mantra, taught in 1973): sun, moon, earth, infinity; infinity, totality, I am Thou. The healing meditation, chanted on one breath per round, with the elbows tucked in and the palms turned up.
- **Sa Ta Na Ma** (Kirtan Kriya): birth, life, death, rebirth. Each syllable is chanted while the thumb touches the index, middle, ring and little fingers in turn. It is sung aloud, whispered and then silent, then whispered and aloud again.

## Breath of Fire, and caution
Breath of Fire is fast, even breathing through the nose, two or three breaths a second, driven by the navel. It is not taught in this app's Kundalini sessions. Teachers advise against it in pregnancy, during menstruation, and for people with high blood pressure, heart disease, epilepsy or a history of panic. If it is ever used, it should be gentle, and stopped at the first sign of dizziness.

## Deep relaxation and the gong
At the end of the class, lie on the back with the palms up and let the body go completely. The gong is played softly, then fuller, then softly again, and the dense, shifting overtones carry the mind into deep rest. Stay lying still for a few minutes after the sound has faded.
