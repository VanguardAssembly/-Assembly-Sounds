// Tibetan Buddhist and Bön texts: Dzogchen, Mahāmudrā, Chöd, the bardo and the Six Yogas.

@ vajra_recitation
title: The vajra recitation: Om Āḥ Hūṃ with the breath
orig: རྡོ་རྗེའི་བཟླས་པ, rdo rje'i bzlas pa, dorje depa (Tibetan); vajrajāpa (Sanskrit)
by: the Guhyasamāja tradition; Nāgārjuna's *Five Stages* (*Pañcakrama*)
date: 9th–10th centuries in India
fam: tibetan
kind: teaching
credit: Summarised in Assembly Sounds' own words.
more: Thomas F. Yarnall, trans., *Tsong Khapa's Great Treatise on the Stages of Mantra*, chapters 11–12 (2013).
more: Lama Yeshe, *The Bliss of Inner Fire* (1998).
about:
In Vajrayāna practice the three syllables *Om*, *Āḥ* and *Hūṃ* stand for the enlightened body, speech and mind of all buddhas. *Om* is white, at the forehead; *Āḥ* is red, at the throat; *Hūṃ* is blue, at the heart. In the vajra recitation they are joined to the natural breath, so that the breath itself becomes mantra. *Om* comes in with the in-breath, *Āḥ* rests in the brief pause, and *Hūṃ* goes out with the out-breath. The breath is never forced, only accompanied.

The practice comes from the completion stage of the Guhyasamāja Tantra, where "vajra recitation" is the first of the five stages taught in the *Pañcakrama*. There it is used to gather the subtle winds into the central channel. Tibetan teachers also give it as a simple, gentle way for anyone to calm the mind. The Vajra Recitation session uses it in this simple form.

## The practice
1. Sit upright and let the breath settle into its own rhythm.
2. As the breath comes in, hear or feel a white *Om*, filling the body with clarity.
3. As the breath rests for a moment, a red *Āḥ*.
4. As the breath goes out, a blue *Hūṃ*, carrying away all tension.
5. Never hold or push the breath: the syllables follow it, not the other way round.
6. In the end let the syllables fade, and rest in the stillness they leave.

@ three_statements
title: The Three Words That Strike the Vital Point
orig: ཚིག་གསུམ་གནད་བརྡེག, tshig gsum gnad brdeg (Tibetan)
by: Garab Dorje; commentary by Patrul Rinpoche, *The Special Teaching of the Wise and Glorious King*
date: Garab Dorje traditionally 1st millennium CE; Patrul Rinpoche (1808–1887)
fam: tibetan
kind: teaching
credit: Assembly Sounds' own rendering from the Tibetan.
more: The Dalai Lama, *Dzogchen: The Heart Essence of the Great Perfection* (2000), with teaching on Patrul Rinpoche's text.
more: John Myrdhin Reynolds, *The Golden Letters* (1996), on Garab Dorje's three statements.
about:
Dzogchen, the "Great Perfection", is the highest teaching of the Nyingma school of Tibetan Buddhism and of Bön. It teaches that the true nature of mind, *rigpa*, is already pure and complete. It does not need to be built up, only recognised. Garab Dorje, said to be its first human teacher, appeared to his disciple Mañjuśrīmitra as he was dying. He left him three short statements that hold the whole path, his "last testament".

In the 19th century the wandering master Patrul Rinpoche wrote a short commentary on them. It is one of the most loved texts in Tibet. To introduce rigpa directly, he tells the student to let the mind relax and then shout a sudden, forceful *PHAT!*, which cuts every thought. In the gap that opens there is only naked awareness, astonished and wide awake. The PHAT session uses this method.

## The three words
1. **Recognise your own nature directly.** Be introduced to the face of awareness itself.
2. **Decide on one thing alone.** Settle on this awareness, and nothing else.
3. **Gain confidence in liberation.** As thoughts arise, they free themselves, like writing on water.

## The introduction (Patrul Rinpoche, rendered)
First, let your mind be at ease, neither scattered nor concentrated, without thought. Then, suddenly, shout a forceful *PHAT!*: fierce, short and sharp. It cuts through everything. In that moment there is nothing at all, only astonishment: a naked, open, wakeful awareness that cannot be described. Recognise it. This is the face of *rigpa*.

## Self-liberation (Patrul Rinpoche, rendered)
Whatever arises, joy or sorrow, a good thought or a bad one, let it free itself in its own place, as a drawing on water disappears as it is drawn. Do not chase it and do not push it away. When thoughts no longer bind you, you have found confidence in liberation.

@ tilopa
title: Tilopa's Six Words and the Song of Mahāmudrā
orig: གནད་ཀྱི་གཟེར་དྲུག, gnad kyi gzer drug, "six nails of the vital point"; the Ganges Mahāmudrā (Tibetan)
by: Tilopa, to his disciple Nāropa
date: 11th century (Tilopa 988–1069)
fam: tibetan
kind: teaching
credit: Assembly Sounds' own rendering from the Tibetan.
more: Thrangu Rinpoche, *The Ganges Mahamudra* (commentary).
more: Ken McLeod, *Wake Up to Your Life* (2001).
more: Dakpo Tashi Namgyal, *Mahāmudrā: The Moonlight* (16th century; trans. Lobsang P. Lhalungpa, 1986).
about:
Tilopa was a wandering Indian *mahāsiddha*, a "great adept", said to have ground sesame seeds for a living and to have received teachings directly from the buddha Vajradhara. His disciple Nāropa, once a great scholar of Nālandā, served him through twelve years of hard tests. Nāropa's student Marpa carried the teachings to Tibet, where Marpa's student Milarepa became Tibet's greatest yogi. This is the Kagyu lineage, and its central teaching is *Mahāmudrā*, the "great seal": resting the mind in its own nature.

Tilopa summed up the way of resting in six short instructions, the "six nails". He also sang a song to Nāropa on the banks of the Ganges. It describes the mind as like space, which rests on nothing, and tells him to let the body be like a hollow bamboo. The Six Words session follows them one by one.

## The six words
= mi mno, mi bsam, mi dpyod, mi sgom, mi sems, rang sar bzhag
1. Don't recall: let go of what has passed.
2. Don't imagine: let go of what may come.
3. Don't think: let go of what is happening now.
4. Don't examine: don't try to work anything out.
5. Don't control: don't try to make anything happen.
6. Rest: relax, right now, as you are.

## From the Song of Mahāmudrā (rendered)
Mahāmudrā cannot be shown. Space rests on nothing; in the same way the mind rests on nothing. Let it relax in its own nature, and do not try to fix it in place.

Do nothing with the body but relax. Keep the mouth closed and silent. Let the mind be empty, thinking of nothing. Like a hollow bamboo, let the body rest at ease. Neither giving nor taking, let the mind rest.

Clouds rise in the sky and dissolve back into it, going nowhere. In the same way thoughts rise in the mind and dissolve back into it. When you see the nature of mind, the waves of thought subside.

A single lamp drives out the darkness of a thousand ages. In the same way a single moment of the mind's clear light ends the ignorance of countless lives.

@ chod
title: Chöd, the practice of cutting, and Feeding Your Demons
orig: གཅོད, gcod, "cutting" (Tibetan)
by: Machig Labdrön; the modern method by Lama Tsultrim Allione
date: Machig 1055–1149; *Feeding Your Demons* 2008
fam: tibetan
kind: teaching
credit: Summarised in Assembly Sounds' own words.
more: Jérôme Edou, *Machig Labdrön and the Foundations of Chöd* (1996).
more: Sarah Harding, trans., *Machik's Complete Explanation: Clarifying the Meaning of Chöd* (2003).
more: Tsultrim Allione, *Feeding Your Demons: Ancient Wisdom for Resolving Inner Conflict* (2008).
about:
Machig Labdrön (1055–1149) was a Tibetan woman, a scholar who as a girl read the long Perfection of Wisdom sūtras faster than any monk. She founded *Chöd*, "cutting", the only Buddhist practice lineage begun in Tibet that later spread back to India. It joins the Perfection of Wisdom's teaching of emptiness with a dramatic ritual of generosity. Often in a charnel ground at night, with a hand drum and a thigh-bone trumpet, the practitioner visualises their own body cut up and transformed into nectar. They offer it to all beings, and above all to the "demons" that harm them, until every guest is satisfied.

What is cut is self-clinging, the ego's grasping. For Machig the real demons are not outside: any clinging is a demon. In 2008 the American teacher Lama Tsultrim Allione drew a five-step method from Chöd, called "feeding your demons". It works with fears, addictions and illnesses as figures you can meet and feed rather than fight. The Feeding Your Demons session follows her five steps, and it is inspired by Chöd rather than being the full ritual.

## Machig on demons (rendered)
What we call a demon is not some great black thing that frightens you when you see it. A demon is whatever blocks liberation. Above all, the demon is clinging to a self. Cut that, and every demon is cut at once.

## The five steps, in brief
1. **Find the demon.** Choose what troubles you, and notice where you feel it in the body: its colour, texture, temperature.
2. **Give it a form.** Let it appear in front of you as a figure, and see it clearly. Ask it: What do you want? What do you really need? How will you feel when you get it?
3. **Become the demon.** Change places, and answer as the demon, from inside its body.
4. **Feed the demon.** Return to yourself. Let your body melt into nectar of exactly what it needs, and feed it until it is completely satisfied. Notice whether an ally appears in its place, and what help it offers.
5. **Rest.** Let the demon, the ally and yourself dissolve, and rest in open awareness.

@ bardo
title: The Bardo Thödol and the dissolution of the elements
orig: བར་དོ་ཐོས་གྲོལ, bar do thos grol, "liberation through hearing in the in-between state" (Tibetan)
by: revealed by Karma Lingpa as a treasure text; attributed to Padmasambhava
date: 14th century
fam: tibetan
kind: liturgy
credit: Assembly Sounds' own rendering and summary from the Tibetan.
more: Gyurme Dorje, trans., *The Tibetan Book of the Dead: First Complete Translation* (2005).
more: Lati Rinbochay & Jeffrey Hopkins, *Death, Intermediate State and Rebirth in Tibetan Buddhism* (1979).
more: W. Y. Evans-Wentz & Kazi Dawa Samdup, *The Tibetan Book of the Dead* (1927), the first English version.
about:
In Tibetan Buddhism a *bardo* is an in-between state. The best known is the passage between death and the next birth. The *Bardo Thödol*, known in the West as "The Tibetan Book of the Dead", is a text read aloud beside a dying or dead person. It guides them through what they will meet: first the clear light of death, then peaceful and wrathful visions, and finally the pull of rebirth. Again and again it urges them to recognise everything they see as the display of their own mind, and so be freed.

The Tibetan teachings describe dying as a series of dissolutions. The elements of the body dissolve one into the next, each with an outer sign and an inner vision. Then come three experiences of white, red and black, and finally the clear light. Practitioners rehearse these stages in meditation so that, at death, they will be familiar. The Dissolution session is such a rehearsal, done gently and with a full return.

## The dissolution of the elements
1. **Earth into water**: the body feels heavy and weak. Inner sign: a shimmering **mirage**, like water on a hot road.
2. **Water into fire**: the mouth and nose grow dry. Inner sign: drifting **smoke**.
3. **Fire into air**: warmth leaves the body. Inner sign: **sparks**, like fireflies in the dark.
4. **Air into consciousness**: the breath stops. Inner sign: a **flickering lamp** about to go out.

## The three appearances and the clear light
1. **White appearance**: like a clear autumn sky lit by moonlight.
2. **Red increase**: like that sky lit red by the sun.
3. **Black attainment**: like the sky on a moonless night, and a swoon.
4. **Clear light**: like the sky at dawn, clear of moonlight, sunlight and darkness. This is the mind's own nature, shining.

## The words read at the moment of death (rendered)
Child of a noble family, listen. Now the pure clear light of reality is dawning before you. Recognise it. Your own awareness, at this moment, empty and clear, without any centre or edge, is itself the Buddha. Do not be afraid, and do not be distracted. Rest in it.

@ six_yogas
title: The Six Yogas of Nāropa
orig: ནཱ་རོ་ཆོས་དྲུག, nā ro chos drug (Tibetan)
by: Nāropa, from the teaching of Tilopa; set out by Tsongkhapa and the Kagyu masters
date: 11th century; Tsongkhapa's *A Book of Three Inspirations* c. 1400
fam: tibetan
kind: manual
credit: Summarised in Assembly Sounds' own words.
more: Glenn H. Mullin, trans., *Tsongkhapa's Six Yogas of Naropa* (1996).
more: Lama Yeshe, *The Bliss of Inner Fire* (1998).
more: Garma C. C. Chang, trans., *Esoteric Teachings of the Tibetan Tantra* (1961).
about:
The Six Yogas are a set of advanced completion-stage practices that Nāropa received from Tilopa and passed through Marpa and Milarepa to the Kagyu and Gelug schools. They work with the subtle body: its channels, winds and drops. Their aim is to turn the most ordinary experiences of a human life into the path of awakening: warmth, the waking world, dreams, deep sleep, death and the passage after death.

The best-known is *tummo*, inner heat. Milarepa mastered it so fully that he wore only a thin cotton cloth through Himalayan winters, which is why he is called "Repa", "the cotton-clad". Dream yoga trains recognition within dreams, and clear-light yoga recognition within deep sleep. Phowa, the transference of consciousness, prepares for the moment of death. Traditionally all six are taught only after long preparation and under a teacher. The sessions in this app offer only gentle, introductory forms.

## The six
1. **Tummo, inner heat**: with vase breathing and visualisation, a flame is kindled at the navel. It rises up the central channel, melts the white drop at the crown, and fills the body with bliss.
2. **Gyulü, illusory body**: seeing the body and the waking world as dreamlike and illusory, like a reflection in a mirror.
3. **Milam, dream yoga**: recognising dreams as dreams while dreaming, and learning to change them, until waking life too is seen as a dream.
4. **Ösel, clear light**: recognising the luminous nature of mind in deep, dreamless sleep, and at death.
5. **Bardo, the in-between state**: training to recognise the stages after death and to be freed in them.
6. **Phowa, transference**: at the moment of death, sending the consciousness, pictured as a drop of light, up through the central channel and out through the crown to a pure land.

## A note on the breath
Traditional tummo uses a strong held breath, called the vase. The app's gentle form uses soft, unstrained breathing with only a light pause, and the forceful form stays behind its warning. Do not practise strong breath retention without a teacher, and never if you are pregnant or have heart or blood-pressure problems.

@ bon_dzogchen
title: Bön Dzogchen: the White A, sky gazing, and the yogas of dream and sleep
orig: ཞང་ཞུང་སྙན་རྒྱུད, zhang zhung snyan rgyud, the Oral Transmission of Zhang Zhung; ཨ་ཁྲིད, a khrid, the A instruction (Tibetan)
by: the Bön tradition: Tapihritsa and Gyerpung Nangzher Lödpo; Meu Gongjad Ritrod Chenpo; Shardza Tashi Gyaltsen; Tenzin Wangyal Rinpoche
date: the Zhang Zhung lineage traditionally 8th century; the A-khrid from the 11th century; modern teachings from 1990s
fam: tibetan
kind: teaching
credit: Summarised in Assembly Sounds' own words.
more: Tenzin Wangyal Rinpoche, *Wonders of the Natural Mind* (1993) and *The Tibetan Yogas of Dream and Sleep* (1998).
more: Per Kværne, *The Bon Religion of Tibet* (1995).
more: John Myrdhin Reynolds, *The Oral Tradition from Zhang-Zhung* (2005).
about:
Bön is Tibet's oldest living religious tradition. Its followers trace it to the teacher Tönpa Shenrab Miwoche, who they say came long before the Buddha from the land of Olmo Lungring in the west. Bön and Tibetan Buddhism shaped each other over a thousand years. Bön keeps its own scriptures, its own lineages and its own Dzogchen, the "Great Perfection". The Dalai Lama recognised it as a fifth school of Tibetan religion.

Its most important Dzogchen teaching is the *Oral Transmission of Zhang Zhung*. In the 8th century, according to tradition, the master Tapihritsa appeared as a radiant child to the hermit Gyerpung Nangzher Lödpo and gave him the teaching. In the 11th century Meu Gongjad Ritrod Chenpo (1038–1096) arranged the practice of the *A-khrid*, "the instruction through A". In it the Tibetan letter A, the sound before all sounds, stands for the unborn nature of mind. The student pictures a white A inside a sphere of five-coloured rainbow light, sounds "Aaa" and then rests in the natural state.

Bön Dzogchen also teaches gazing into the open sky to mix the mind with space, and practices for staying aware in dreams and in deep sleep. Tenzin Wangyal Rinpoche brought these to the West. The White A, sky-gazing, dream-yoga and sleep-yoga sessions follow this tradition.

## The White A
1. Sit upright and breathe out stale air.
2. Picture, at the heart or in the space in front of you, a white Tibetan letter A, shining, inside a sphere of five-coloured light: white, green, red, blue and yellow.
3. Sound a long, open "Aaa", and feel it spread through the whole body and out into space.
4. Let the image dissolve into light, and the light into space. Rest in the open awareness that remains, without adding anything.

## Sky gazing
Sit facing a clear, open sky, with the sun behind you. Let the eyes rest, open, gazing into space without fixing on anything. Let the breath be soft. Feel the space outside, the space of the body and the space of the mind as one, with no inside and no outside. If thoughts arise, let them dissolve into the sky like clouds.

## The yogas of dream and sleep, in brief
- Through the day, keep reminding yourself, "This is a dream."
- Before sleep, set a strong intention to recognise your dreams.
- Fall asleep with the attention resting gently on a point of light at the heart or the throat.
- When you wake in the night, renew the intention.
- In sleep yoga, the aim goes further: to fall asleep without losing awareness, so that even dreamless sleep is recognised as the mind's clear light.

## The Ma Tri mantra
= Om ma tri mu ye sa le du
The essence mantra of Bön, recited as Buddhists recite *Om mani padme hum*.

@ gyuto
title: The chant of the Gyuto Tantric College
orig: རྒྱུད་སྟོད, rgyud stod, Gyutö, "Upper Tantric College" (Tibetan)
by: the monks of Gyuto and Gyume; acoustic study by Huston Smith, Kenneth N. Stevens and Raymond S. Tomlinson
date: Gyuto founded 1474; the study 1967
fam: tibetan
kind: study
cite: H. Smith, K. N. Stevens & R. S. Tomlinson, "On an unusual mode of chanting by certain Tibetan lamas", *Journal of the Acoustical Society of America* 41 (1967): 1262–1264.
credit: Summarised in Assembly Sounds' own words.
more: The Gyuto Monks, *Tibetan Tantric Choir* (recording, 1987) and *Freedom Chants from the Roof of the World* (1989).
more: Huston Smith, *The World's Religions* (1991), on Tibetan Buddhism.
about:
The Gyuto and Gyume colleges were founded in Lhasa in the 15th century as the two great tantric colleges of the Gelug school. Gyume was founded in 1433 and Gyuto in 1474. After 1959 their monks rebuilt them in exile in India. They are famous for a deep, rumbling style of chant in which each monk seems to sing a chord with one voice. Under a very low fundamental note a clear upper tone rings out, two octaves and a third higher.

In 1964 the religion scholar Huston Smith heard and recorded the monks in India. He then worked with two acousticians at MIT to analyse the sound. They found that the monks shape the throat and mouth so as to strongly reinforce one upper harmonic of an extremely low voice. The monks say the style was revealed in a dream to the college's founder, and that it is the voice of the wrathful deities. The Deep Chant session builds a drone of this kind.

## What the study found, in brief
- The fundamental note was very low, around the bottom of the male voice.
- One upper harmonic, about two octaves and a major third above it, was made unusually strong, so it is heard as a separate note.
- The effect comes from the shaping of the vocal tract, not from two separate sounds in the throat.

@ kyerim
title: The generation and completion stages of deity yoga
orig: བསྐྱེད་རིམ, bskyed rim, kyerim; རྫོགས་རིམ, rdzogs rim, dzogrim (Tibetan); utpattikrama, sampannakrama (Sanskrit)
by: Indian and Tibetan Vajrayāna tradition
date: from the 8th century
fam: tibetan
kind: teaching
credit: Summarised in Assembly Sounds' own words.
more: Jamgön Kongtrül, *Creation and Completion: Essential Points of Tantric Meditation* (trans. Sarah Harding, 2002).
more: The Dalai Lama & Jeffrey Hopkins, *Deity Yoga* (1981).
about:
Almost every Tibetan Buddhist practice with a deity, whether Chenrezig, Tārā, the Medicine Buddha, Vajrasattva or Mañjuśrī, follows the same pattern, called a *sādhana*, "means of accomplishment". In the *generation stage* you dissolve your ordinary sense of self into emptiness. From that emptiness you arise in imagination as the deity, or see the deity before you. You recite the mantra while lights stream out to all beings and back. In the *completion stage* everything dissolves again into emptiness, and you rest in the nature of mind.

The point is not to worship an outside god. It is to become familiar with your own enlightened qualities, pictured as the deity, until you recognise them as your own. The visualisation sessions and the deity sessions follow this shape in a simplified form.

## The shape of a practice
1. **Refuge and intention**: go for refuge, and resolve to practise for the benefit of all beings.
2. **Emptiness**: dissolve everything, yourself included, into open, empty space, with the words below.
3. **Arising**: from emptiness a seed syllable of light appears, and becomes the deity.
4. **The three essentials**: see the deity clearly; feel the deity's dignity and confidence as your own; remember that the form is empty and pure, like a rainbow.
5. **Mantra**: recite the mantra, picturing it circling at the deity's heart, sending out light that purifies all beings and returns.
6. **Dissolution**: the deity and its world melt into light, and the light into space. Rest in that.
7. **Dedication**: offer whatever good has come from the practice to the welfare of all.

## The words of emptiness
= Oṃ svabhāva-śuddhāḥ sarva-dharmāḥ svabhāva-śuddho 'ham
All things are pure by nature; I too am pure by nature.
