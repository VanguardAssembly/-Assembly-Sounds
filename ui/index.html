What the research actually says

  • How it works. Play two slightly different tones, one in each ear (say 200 Hz left, 206 Hz right), and the brainstem hears a "beat" at the difference: 6 Hz. Monaural beats mix both tones before they reach your ears. Isochronic tones switch a single tone on and off at the beat rate, which works without headphones and above 40 Hz.
  • Evidence labels. Every preset is tagged Research (a published study), Gateway (an interpretation of the Monroe model), Traditional (a popular association), or Speculative (a fringe claim, included for completeness).
  • Gateway Process. A 1983 U.S. Army analysis (McDonnell), declassified by the CIA in 2003, was a theoretical review, not a controlled trial. Monroe's real frequency mixes are proprietary, so these presets are interpretations.
  • Evidence. A 2019 meta-analysis (Garcia-Argibay et al.) found small-to-moderate effects on anxiety, memory, attention and pain. Several EEG studies (e.g. Orozco Perez et al., 2020) found little sign that brainwaves actually lock onto the beat.
  • Altered States group. These sessions are ranked by how much research exists: hypnosis (49 meta-analyses), Ganzfeld (decades of EEG/fMRI), rhythmic drumming trance (fMRI), targeted lucidity reactivation, slow-wave sleep pulses, Kirtan Kriya (RCT), singing bowls, mantra rhythm (BMJ), nature soundscapes and Om chanting (fMRI). Flicker-light, infrasound and hyperventilation methods are deliberately left out because they can cause harm.
  • Bottom line. Treat this as a good tool for relaxation and meditation, not as a guaranteed way to change your state of consciousness, and not as medicine.
